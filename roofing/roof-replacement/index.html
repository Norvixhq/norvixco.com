Roof Replacement Done Properly

Tear off, look at the deck, fix what is under there, and build the system back correctly. A replacement is only as good as the parts nobody sees.

A replacement is not just new shingles. It is a system: decking, underlayment, ice-and-water protection at the vulnerable spots, drip edge, valley treatment, flashing, penetration boots, ventilation, and then the shingles on top. Skip any one of those and the roof will tell on itself in a few years.

The most useful moment of any replacement is the hour after tear-off, when the deck is exposed. Soft plywood, old leaks, rot around a chimney, or a previous repair done badly — that is when it becomes visible. We photograph it and talk to you before we cover it back up.

When roof replacement is worth looking into

Widespread hail bruising

When impact damage is spread across multiple slopes rather than concentrated in one area, spot repair stops making sense.

Repeated repairs in different places

One leak is an event. Three leaks in three seasons is a roof telling you it is finished.

Bare or heavily worn shingle mat

Once granule loss exposes the asphalt, UV goes to work on the mat directly and the countdown accelerates.

Sagging rooflines or soft decking

A visible dip usually means the structure or the deck underneath has taken on moisture.

Discontinued or unmatchable shingles

Sometimes repair is technically fine but a color match is impossible, and the decision becomes an appearance question.

Causes

What causes it

Accumulated storm damage

Several seasons of hail and wind stack up, and a roof that survived each individual storm reaches the end together.

Age and UV exposure

Sun does the slow work. South and west-facing slopes almost always age first in Texas.

Poor original installation

Nailing above the line, missing starter strip, or no valley protection will cut years off an otherwise good product.

Ventilation failure

Superheated attic air degrades shingles from underneath and can void material coverage.

Our inspection

How we look at it

  • Full condition assessment by a licensed inspector, documented with photos
  • Slope-by-slope evaluation so damage patterns are recorded accurately
  • Penetration, flashing, and valley review — these decide most replacements
  • Attic check for ventilation, moisture, and deck condition where accessible
  • Measurement and material takeoff for an itemized written estimate

North Texas conditions

Replacements cluster in North Texas after spring hail season, which means the busiest crews in the Metroplex are often the least careful ones. A replacement scheduled properly, with the deck actually inspected and the flashing actually replaced, is worth waiting a few days for.

In detail

What a replacement actually replaces

Homeowners are quoted shingles. What they are buying is a system, and the shingles are the last few hours of it. Underneath sits the decking, the underlayment, the ice-and-water membrane at the vulnerable points, the drip edge at the perimeter, the valley treatment, the flashing at every wall and chimney, fresh boots on every penetration, and the ventilation that lets the whole assembly breathe.

Any one of those can be skipped without a homeowner ever noticing on completion day. Reused flashing looks identical from the driveway. A missing starter course is invisible. Valley membrane that was left out shows up in year six as a stain on a bedroom ceiling. This is the entire reason estimates from different companies can differ by thousands of dollars while describing what sounds like the same job.

When you compare quotes, compare those lines rather than the headline number. If an estimate does not name the underlayment, say whether flashing and boots are new or reused, or account for decking repair, it is not describing the same work.

The hour after tear-off is the most useful hour of the job

Once the old roofing is off, the deck is exposed and telling the truth for the first time in twenty years. Soft or delaminated plywood, dark staining around a chimney, a patch where somebody fixed a leak by nailing a scrap over it, sheathing that was never fastened properly to begin with — all of it becomes visible at once, and only for a few hours.

We photograph what is there and show you before anything gets covered. If sheathing needs replacing, you see the specific sheets and get the cost before we proceed. That is the opposite of how decking commonly appears on an invoice, which is as a lump sum after the fact.

It is also why we do not recommend roofing over an existing layer. A layover skips this step entirely, adds weight the structure was not designed for, traps heat against the new shingles, and makes the next tear-off more expensive. It saves money once and costs money twice.

Why complexity matters more than square footage

Two roofs of identical area can differ substantially in both price and risk. A simple hip roof over a single-story ranch is mostly field — large uninterrupted planes where the work is repetitive and forgiving. A cut-up roofline with six gables, three dormers, two chimneys, and a dozen penetrations is mostly edges, and edges are where roofs leak.

Newer North Texas construction trends heavily toward the second kind. It photographs beautifully from the street, and it means valleys, transitions, and flashing details drive both the labour and the long-term outcome. When you are comparing estimates on a complex roof, the line items that matter are the ones covering valley treatment and flashing, not the shingle brand.

Choosing a colour you will still like in fifteen years

Shingle colour is the one decision that is genuinely yours, and the one most often made in a hurry from a small sample board in bad light. We bring physical samples and hold them against your actual brick, stone, and trim, in daylight, on the elevation people see from the street.

A few things worth knowing before you choose. Blends read differently at full scale than on a sample — a shingle that looks strongly brown in your hand often reads as neutral across a whole roof. Darker roofs run hotter, though attic ventilation and insulation influence your energy bill far more than colour does. And if you are in an HOA, check the approved palette before falling in love with something.

From our inspections

What we keep finding on North Texas roofs

Flashing reused to save an hour

Old step and counter flashing left in place under new shingles. It was corroded before the new roof went on and it will outlive nothing.

Nails driven above the nail line

High nailing leaves the fastener holding one layer instead of two. The roof passes a glance and fails in the first serious wind.

No valley membrane

The highest-volume part of the roof, built without the protection underneath that the manufacturer specifies.

Pipe boots caulked, not replaced

A ten-dollar part skipped on a five-figure job, then sealed over. It cracks again within a couple of Texas summers.

Exhaust vents added, intake ignored

More vents on the roof with the soffits still packed with insulation. The attic cannot breathe and the new shingles age early from underneath.

Debris left in the gutters

Tear-off grit packed into the troughs, so the first heavy rain after a brand-new roof produces an overflow at the foundation.

Questions

Roof Replacement FAQs

Can you roof over the existing shingles?

We do not recommend it. A layover hides deck problems, adds weight, traps heat, and makes the next replacement more expensive. Tear-off is how you find out what is really under there.

What happens if you find rotted decking?

We stop, photograph it, show you, and price the repair before we proceed. You will not get a surprise line item after the fact.

How much noise and disruption should I expect?

Tear-off day is genuinely loud, and things on shelves can vibrate. Most homeowners find it easiest to plan around it, especially with small children or anyone working from home.

Do I need to be home during the work?

Not usually, though we like you there for the final walkthrough. We will keep you updated either way.

What about my landscaping and driveway?

They get protected before tear-off starts, and we run magnets over the yard, beds, and drive at the end. Nails in tires are the most common complaint about roofing crews, and it is entirely preventable.
