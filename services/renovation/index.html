The handoff problem

The most common failure point in a restoration project is not the drying and it is not the rebuild. It is the seam between them.

A mitigation company removes materials and leaves. You are handed a list and told to find a contractor. That contractor did not see what was behind the wall, is working from someone else's scope, and has no reason to agree with it. Your adjuster now has two parties describing the same job differently. Meanwhile you are living in a house with open walls waiting for a bid.

Carrying both halves eliminates that seam. Tear-out is scoped with the rebuild in mind, one scope goes to the adjuster, and the timeline does not stall while a second contractor is sourced.

What we rebuild

  • Framing and structural repair — studs, joists, subfloor and decking.
  • Insulation, drywall and texture — including matching existing texture, which is the detail that determines whether a repair reads as a repair.
  • Flooring — hardwood repair and refinishing, LVP, tile, laminate and carpet.
  • Kitchens — cabinetry, countertops, backsplash, plumbing and appliance reconnection.
  • Bathrooms — vanities, tile, shower and tub surrounds, fixtures and waterproofing done properly.
  • Interior finishes — trim, baseboards, crown, doors, hardware and paint.
  • Exterior — siding, soffit, fascia, decking and fencing.
  • Systems coordination — licensed electrical, plumbing and HVAC subcontractors where the scope requires it.

Rebuilding to a claim

Insurance reconstruction is a specific discipline. The scope has to be written in line-item format the carrier's estimating software recognizes, matching materials and finishes have to be justified, and supplements have to be documented when hidden damage appears once walls are open — which happens on most jobs. We handle that documentation rather than asking you to argue it.

One point worth knowing: many policies include ordinance or law coverage, which pays for bringing affected areas up to current building code even where the original construction was compliant when built. On older DFW homes this comes up regularly with electrical and plumbing, and it is frequently overlooked.

Renovations that are not insurance jobs

We also take on straightforward remodels — a kitchen, a bathroom, flooring throughout, a whole-house refresh before a sale. And when a loss has already opened a wall, it is often the most economical moment to make a change you were planning anyway. We will tell you plainly what falls inside the claim scope and what is a betterment you are paying for, because that line has to be clear before work starts.

Insurance rebuild and elective renovation are different jobs

We do both, and it helps to be clear about which one you are having.

Insurance reconstruction restores what was there, to a scope agreed with your carrier, priced in the estimating format carriers work from. The scope is defined by the loss, and the constraint is what the policy covers.

Elective renovation is you deciding to change something. The constraint is your budget and your intent.

They intersect constantly, because a loss frequently opens up an area that a household has wanted to change anyway. That works well — the demolition is already done and paid for — but the accounting has to stay clean. Covered restoration work goes on the claim; upgrades are quoted separately and paid separately. Blending the two produces a claim that gets questioned and a homeowner who cannot tell what they are paying for. We keep two columns.

Code upgrades and ordinance or law

Older DFW properties routinely cannot be rebuilt exactly as they were, because current code has moved. Common triggers are GFCI and AFCI protection requirements, receptacle spacing, older wiring types, plumbing that no longer meets code, egress window sizing in bedrooms, and insulation values.

Standard policies pay to restore what was there, not to upgrade it. Ordinance or law coverage pays the difference. Many homeowners carry it without knowing, and it is one of the most commonly missed items on a rebuild claim. We flag every code-driven cost we encounter so you can put the question to your carrier rather than absorbing it silently.

Matching what already exists

The hardest part of a partial rebuild is making the new part disappear into the old part. Flooring gets discontinued. Cabinet lines change. Paint fades, so a factory-matched colour does not match a wall that has been on the sun side of the house for nine years. Wall and ceiling texture is applied by hand and every applicator's pattern is slightly different.

Where a seamless match is not achievable, we tell you before the work rather than after, and lay out the options — extending the scope to a natural break line, replacing a full elevation instead of a section, or accepting a visible transition. Carriers will often fund a reasonable extension to achieve continuity when the case is made properly at scoping time. Made after installation, it rarely goes anywhere.

Permits and inspections

Structural work, electrical, plumbing and mechanical work generally require permits, and requirements vary between Dallas, Fort Worth, Plano, Frisco and every other jurisdiction we work in. We pull permits in our name and manage inspections. Work done without a required permit is a problem that surfaces at resale, when the buyer's inspector asks for documentation that does not exist.

Sequence, and what actually determines the timeline

Rebuild runs in order: rough trades, inspection, insulation, drywall, texture, paint, flooring, cabinetry and trim, fixtures and finish, final inspection, final clean. Steps cannot be usefully compressed, and skipping ahead of an inspection means undoing work.

The realistic variables are material lead times — cabinetry and specialty flooring run weeks — municipal inspection scheduling, and how quickly claim approvals come back. We order long-lead items as early in the process as approval allows, because a rebuild waiting on cabinets that were ordered late is the most avoidable delay in this trade.

Working in an occupied home

Most renovation work happens in a house people are still living in, and how a job is run matters as much as how it is built. Work areas are separated with dust barriers rather than a sheet over the doorway, floors on the route in and out are protected, tools and materials are consolidated at the end of each day rather than left across the house, and the site is left swept.

Work hours are agreed at the start and kept to. If you have someone working from home, a small child on a nap schedule or a night-shift worker, tell us before the job rather than during it — noisy trades can usually be sequenced around a constraint we know about in advance.

Cost

What drives the price of renovation & rebuild

Reconstruction cost is driven by finish level and scope, and by how much of it your policy covers. We give you a written estimate free, before any work begins — but these are the factors that move the number, so you know what you are being quoted on.

Scope and square footage

How many rooms, and whether structural, electrical or plumbing work is involved.

Finish level

Builder-grade replacement versus custom cabinetry, tile and millwork.

Code upgrades

Work current code requires that the original construction did not include.

Permits and inspections

Required for structural, electrical, plumbing and mechanical work, and priced into the job.

Material lead times

Not a cost in itself, but long-lead cabinetry and flooring drive the schedule.

Matching existing finishes

Where a seamless match needs the scope extended to a natural break line.

We do not publish fixed prices, because a number quoted without seeing the property would be a guess. What we will do is walk the job with you, explain what we have found, and put the scope in writing at no cost.
