What is different about working in someone's home

The technical work is the same. Everything around it is not. In a house there are children, pets, work schedules, a kitchen someone needs to use tonight and belongings that matter for reasons no inventory captures. Residential restoration done well means treating the property as occupied space rather than a job site: floor protection and containment, clear daily communication about what is happening and when, equipment placed to minimize disruption where that does not compromise the drying, and a straight answer to the question everyone asks first — can we stay here?

What we handle in homes

  • Water damage from burst pipes, water heaters, appliances, slab leaks and roof intrusion
  • Fire and smoke damage, including board-up, soot removal and odor elimination
  • Mold inspection, containment and remediation
  • Storm, hail and wind damage with emergency tarping
  • Flood and sewage backup cleanup
  • Contents cleaning, pack-out and storage
  • Junk removal and debris haul-off
  • Full reconstruction through final paint

Can you stay in the house?

Usually the answer is yes when the loss is contained to one area, the kitchen and at least one bathroom are usable, there is no contamination or significant mold, and power and HVAC are functioning. The answer tends toward no when the affected area includes the only bathroom or the kitchen, when there is Category 3 water or significant mold, when structural safety is in question, or when someone in the household has a respiratory condition that the work would aggravate.

We will give you that assessment early rather than at 9pm on the first night, so you can make arrangements. If you do need to relocate, your policy's additional living expense coverage typically reimburses the difference between your normal costs and what you are spending — keep every receipt.

One project lead

You get a single person who knows your job, coordinates the crews and the adjuster, and answers when you call. Not a dispatcher, not a different technician each morning who has to be told the history again.

Living in the house while we work

Most residential losses do not require you to move out, and staying put saves money and disruption. It does require the job to be run differently than an empty property would be.

Work areas are separated from living areas with hard containment — plastic barriers on framing with zippered access, not a sheet taped across a doorway. Negative air machines with HEPA filtration hold the work area at lower pressure than the rest of the house, so air moves into the containment rather than out of it, and dust and any contamination stay where they are generated. Walk-off matting and protected pathways keep traffic off clean flooring.

Equipment noise is the part people underestimate. Air movers and dehumidifiers run 24 hours a day and they are loud. Where we can, we shift equipment placement in the evening and consolidate machines in rooms furthest from bedrooms. Where the drying calculation will not allow it, we tell you in advance rather than letting you discover it at 11pm.

When you should not stay

We will tell you plainly, on the first visit, if the property is not safe to occupy — and we will tell you early enough to make arrangements in daylight rather than at bedtime. The usual reasons are sewage contamination, active mold in occupied areas, fire and smoke residue with compromised air quality, loss of a functioning kitchen or the only bathroom, structural concerns, or an HVAC system that cannot run.

Households with infants, elderly members, or anyone with respiratory or immune vulnerability get a more conservative recommendation, because the threshold for them is genuinely lower.

If you do relocate, keep every receipt. Additional living expense coverage generally reimburses the reasonable extra cost of living elsewhere — lodging, meals above your normal spend, pet boarding, extra mileage — but it reimburses what you can document.

Children and pets

Both are affected more than people expect. Equipment is loud and strange, containment blocks familiar routes through the house, and there are unfamiliar adults in the property for days. We work with you on scheduling around nap times and school runs where it is possible, and we ask that pets are secured away from work areas — partly for their safety around equipment and open materials, and partly because a door held open for a hose run is a door a dog will find.

Phasing so the house stays livable

Where the loss affects multiple areas, sequencing matters more than raw speed. Bathrooms and the kitchen are prioritised because a household without either becomes untenable quickly. Bedrooms come back before living areas. Garage and storage areas go last.

On a rebuild, we sequence to keep at least one functional bathroom available at all times where the layout permits it, even when it would be faster to gut both at once.

The transition to rebuild

The awkward moment on most residential jobs is the gap between mitigation finishing and reconstruction starting — the period where the house has open walls, missing flooring, and nobody scheduled. It happens because two different companies are involved and each is waiting on the carrier or on each other.

We carry both phases, which removes the gap entirely. The drying scope is written with the rebuild in mind, materials are ordered while drying is still running, and the reconstruction crew starts when the moisture readings clear rather than when a new company gets round to estimating.

Communication during the job

The complaint we hear most often about other restoration companies is not about workmanship. It is that nobody told the homeowner what was happening. Crews arrived unannounced, equipment appeared and disappeared, and the person living in the house found out about scope changes by noticing them.

You get one point of contact for the whole job, mitigation through rebuild. You are told before anyone arrives, told what is happening that day, and told immediately if we find something that changes the scope — hidden damage behind a wall, mold, a code issue — rather than at the end when it appears on an invoice.

Nothing outside the agreed scope proceeds without your approval and, where insurance is involved, your carrier's. That is a slower way to run a job and it is the only way that leaves people with an accurate understanding of what they are paying for.

Warranty and what happens afterward

Reconstruction work carries a written workmanship warranty, and manufacturer warranties on materials pass through to you with the documentation. At completion you receive a closing package — the drying logs, the photographic record, the final scope and the warranty terms — which is worth keeping, both for a future claim and for the buyer's inspector at resale.

If something is wrong afterward, call and we will come back. A local company with a physical address in Dallas can be held to that in a way that a crew working the Metroplex out of a truck cannot.

Cost

What drives the price of residential restoration

A whole-home job is the sum of its phases, and the phases are priced separately. We give you a written estimate free, before any work begins — but these are the factors that move the number, so you know what you are being quoted on.

Mitigation scope

Extraction, drying, removal and cleaning in the first days.

Contents handling

Whether belongings stay in place, get moved within the property, or are packed out.

Occupancy

Working around a household requires containment and phasing, which takes longer than an empty property.

Reconstruction scope

How much has to be rebuilt, and to what finish level.

Claim coverage

What your policy pays versus what falls to you, including your deductible.

We do not publish fixed prices, because a number quoted without seeing the property would be a guess. What we will do is walk the job with you, explain what we have found, and put the scope in writing at no cost.
