Lease vs finance vs cash — the real difference

All three routes put the same car on the same driveway. What differs is what you are paying for and what you are left holding when the term ends:

  • Lease — you pay the depreciation the car suffers while you have it, plus a rent charge set by the “money factor”. You are financing a slice of the car, not all of it. At the end you hand it back and own nothing.
  • Finance — you pay off the whole car plus interest. The payment is higher, but every dollar of principal buys equity. Our auto loan calculator splits that payment month by month.
  • Cash — no interest at all, and the lowest headline cost in almost every comparison. But it commits capital that could be working elsewhere.

A car is two things at once: a service you consume and an asset you hold. Everybody consumes the same service. What a lease-versus-buy decision settles is whether you also want the asset, and what you will pay to avoid it. Depreciation ties the two together and is unavoidable either way — the buyer pays it silently as a lower resale value, the lessee explicitly as a line in the payment. Our depreciation calculator and depreciation guides cover how fast different body styles shed value, because that number drives most of what follows.

The trap: comparing monthly payments
A lease almost always shows the lowest monthly payment — that's the whole appeal. But a low payment on something you'll never own is not the same as a low cost. Compare the true total cost and what you hold at the end.

How a lease payment is actually built

A lease payment has exactly two components, and most advertised offers hide which is which. The split tells you which numbers on a worksheet are negotiable. Here is the benchmark car line by line: a $34,000 SUV, 36 months, $2,000 down, 57% residual, 6.0% lease rate, 7% sales tax.

Where the number 2,400 comes from

The instruction to multiply a money factor by 2,400 is repeated everywhere and explained almost nowhere. Over the term the amount outstanding falls in a straight line from the adjusted capitalised cost to the residual, so the average balance is the midpoint: ($32,000 + $19,380) ÷ 2, or $25,690. A monthly rate is the APR divided by 1,200. Multiply that by the average balance and you have the rent charge — the same as multiplying the sum of the two figures by the APR divided by 2,400. The 2,400 is 1,200 for the months, doubled to take the average.

That also explains what catches people out: the rent charge is flat, $128.45 in month one and $128.45 in month 36. A loan's interest falls as the balance amortizes; a lease's does not, because the arithmetic has already averaged it. Lease rent is not cheaper than loan interest, merely levelled. And because it is quoted as a decimal, the money factor is where markup hides most easily:

The four numbers that rebuild any lease quote

A dealer can quote a monthly payment without disclosing how it was reached, and a payment alone is unauditable. Four figures let you reconstruct it yourself, and there is no good reason to be refused any of them:

  • The selling price. This is the capitalised cost. Negotiate it exactly as you would a cash purchase, and settle it before leasing is mentioned at all.
  • The money factor, as a decimal. Multiply by 2,400 and set the result against the loan APR you were quoted elsewhere. A dealer may mark it up over the lender's buy rate within your credit tier.
  • The residual, as both a percentage and a dollar figure. You cannot move it, but comparing it with a realistic three-year valuation tells you whether the manufacturer is subsidizing the deal.
  • Every fee, itemized. Acquisition, disposition, documentation — and, critically, whether any has been rolled into the capitalised cost, where it quietly attracts rent charge for the whole term.

Put those four into the worksheet above and the quoted payment should reproduce to within a dollar. If it does not, something has been added that you have not been shown — and the discrepancy, not the payment, is the thing to ask about.

Cash down on a lease is not a down payment
A capitalised cost reduction is prepaid rent, not equity. If the car is written off in month four, the insurer pays the leasing company and your $2,000 is generally gone — you hold no ownership interest to claim it against. On a purchase, a down payment survives as equity in the settlement. Put as little cash into a lease as the deal allows, and check that gap cover is included.

The residual value is the whole game

Residual value is the price at which the lender agrees, today, to take the car back in three years. It is the largest single determinant of your payment and the one number you cannot negotiate. On a subvented deal the manufacturer sets it deliberately high to make a payment look attractive. Watch the same car, same price, same money factor, same term, when only the residual moves:

ResidualBuyout priceDeprec. / moRent / moPaymentTotal 36-mo cost

Fourteen points of residual is $128.75 a month and $4,635 over the term. Note that the rent charge moves the wrong way: a higher residual raises the average balance, so rent climbs $11.90 a month even as depreciation falls $132.22. The net is strongly in your favor, but the two components are not independent.

Now the part usually left out. Our model puts this car at $19,652 after three years — $34,000 losing 20% in year one and 15% in each of the next two. Set that against the three buyouts:

  • 50% residual, $17,000 buyout. The car is worth $2,652 more than you can buy it for: real, capturable equity at lease end. It also signals the lender expects this model to fall harder than average.
  • 57% residual, $19,380 buyout. Buyout and market value are $272 apart. Nothing to harvest either way. This is what an honestly-set, unsubsidised residual looks like.
  • 64% residual, $21,760 buyout. The car is worth $2,108 less than the buyout. You would never exercise the option. The manufacturer has promised to absorb that $2,108 at auction and handed you the benefit as a lower payment.

That is a subvented lease, and it is the honest answer to when leasing beats buying. Not temperament, not discipline: whether a manufacturer has chosen to buy down the residual on that model in that month. When they have, you pay less than the car's real depreciation and someone else covers the gap. When they have not, leasing is a dearer way to consume the same depreciation. Retention varies enormously by body style — pickups typically hold 55–65% of value over five years, mainstream sedans 40–50%, EVs 30–45% — which is why lease deals cluster on some models and are absent on others. A worked example

A $34,000 car over 3 years — leased ($2,000 down, 57% residual, 6% lease rate) vs financed ($3,400 down, 7.2% APR) vs cash, at 12,000 miles a year and 7% sales tax:

Lease~$513~$22,240Own nothing
Finance~$1,043~$21,300Car worth ~$19,650
Cash—~$17,430Car worth ~$19,650

The lease has by far the lowest payment ($513 vs $1,043) — yet it is the most expensive way to get this car over three years, because you end up with nothing. Financing costs about $940 less and leaves a car worth ~$19,650.

Notice how narrow that lease-versus-finance gap is: $944 over three years, or $26 a month, against a payment difference of $530. Financing is not dramatically cheaper as a way of consuming a car. What separates them is almost entirely the $19,652 on the buyer's side of the ledger — and that only matters if the buyer keeps the car long enough to use it.

What a lease really costs over the same period

Payment-versus-payment is the wrong comparison. So, in truth, is one three-year lease against a five-year loan, because they do not cover the same span of your life. Fix a period, add up every dollar that leaves your account, then ask what you hold at the end.

Six years is the natural horizon: two complete lease cycles, one comfortable ownership cycle. Same car both ways, benchmark terms, second lease written identically. Fuel, insurance and registration are excluded because they are identical on both sides.

The outlays are within $878 of each other. Averaged over 72 months the lessee spends $618 a month and the buyer $606 — twelve dollars apart. On any measure of affordability these are the same path. The entire distinction, $12,946 of it, is the car in the driveway at the end. ($12,069 is the site's curve carried one year past its five-year value of $14,199: another 15% off.)

The shape differs too. The lessee pays $512.54 for 72 months and then, if they carry on leasing, forever. The buyer pays $670.09 for 60 months and then nothing. Those twelve payment-free months are where buying pulls ahead, and they are the years a serial lessee never reaches.

Two caveats. The buyer takes on repair risk in years four to six once the warranty expires; our benchmark budgets $1,588 a year for maintenance, repairs and tires averaged across five years, and the back half carries more than its share. The lessee is under warranty throughout. Against that, the second lease assumes an identical car at an identical price in three years, which recent history suggests is optimistic. Neither adjustment closes a $12,946 gap.

For context, the full five-year picture for this car — fuel, insurance and servicing included — is $58,928, or $0.98 a mile and $11,786 a year: depreciation $19,801, insurance $12,480, fuel $8,000, maintenance $7,941, interest $6,525, taxes and fees $4,180. Normalized to AAA's 15,000-mile assumption that is $12,186 a year, about 5% above AAA's Your Driving Costs 2025 average of $11,577. Run your own car through the true cost to own calculator or the cost per mile calculator and you will see how little of that total a lease removes: it changes the depreciation and finance lines and leaves insurance, fuel and tires where they were.

Mileage limits and the excess-mileage charge

A lease is priced against an assumed mileage. Exceed it and you pay for the extra depreciation you caused, at a rate fixed in the contract — typically 15 to 30 cents a mile. Our benchmark uses 25 cents against a 12,000-mile allowance. The charge arrives as a single bill when you return the car, which is why it surprises people.

Consider the middle row. AAA's average driver covers 15,000 miles a year. On a standard 12,000-mile lease at 25 cents, that entirely ordinary driver hands over $2,250 at the end — more than the $944 by which financing already beat leasing over three years. At 20,000 miles a year the bill is $6,000, some 27% on top of the whole $22,241 cost of the lease.

You can buy a higher allowance at signing, normally priced below the post-hoc rate because the lender would rather have the money than the argument. But prepaid miles are not refunded, so it is a bet on your own driving; check three years of service records before guessing.

Hence the clearest rule on the page. If you drive materially more than the allowance you can afford to buy, do not lease. High-mileage drivers consume depreciation faster than any lease prices it, and they pay twice: once in the overage bill, again in never owning an asset.

Wear, disposition and early termination

Every advertised lease payment describes the middle of the contract. The costs at either end are absent from the advertisement, and they are where the gap between expected and actual cost opens up.

  • The acquisition fee. Charged at signing, paid up front or rolled into the capitalised cost. Typical figures run in the $595 to $1,095 range; our benchmark uses $695. Capitalise it rather than pay it and you also pay rent charge on it all term.
  • The disposition fee. Charged when you hand the car back, commonly $350 to $500; the benchmark uses $395. Normally waived if you buy the car or lease another from the same brand, which is precisely why it exists.
  • Excess wear. The contract defines acceptable condition and bills anything beyond it: curbed alloys, a cracked windscreen, tires below the contractual tread depth. Tires are the one to plan for — a set fitted in month 30 costs far less than the lender's charge in month 36.
  • Early termination. The expensive one. Walking away early generally means settling the remaining payments plus the gap between market value and the adjusted payoff. A lease builds no equity, so nothing on your side offsets it.

The asymmetry is worth naming plainly. A financed car can be sold at any time; the proceeds settle the loan and the remainder is yours. A lease has no such exit. That inflexibility is a real cost even though it never appears on a worksheet, and it should weigh on anyone whose job, household or commute might change inside three years. Our buying guides cover disposal and trade-in timing.

Paying cash, and where opportunity cost belongs

The lease-or-buy framing quietly assumes you are financing something. For anyone with the capital, cash is the third option, and on the benchmark car it is comfortably cheapest: $22,881 over five years, being $19,801 of depreciation plus $2,380 of sales tax and $700 in doc and title fees. Financing the same car costs $29,407 on the same basis. The $6,525 difference is precisely the loan interest.

That is the arithmetic, not the whole decision, because $37,080 handed to a dealer is $37,080 doing nothing else. Tick the opportunity-cost box and the calculator charges the cash buyer the compounded return they gave up. At 5% a year over five years that is $10,245, turning cash from the cheapest option into the dearest. Set the forgone return equal to the $6,525 of interest and the break-even is roughly 3.3% a year.

We would rather explain that model's limits than let the number be over-read. It charges opportunity cost on the full $37,080 held static for five years, while the financed buyer also commits $3,400 up front and repays principal monthly, none of which is credited back. The loan's average outstanding balance is about $17,564, not $33,680, so a strict like-for-like comparison would push the break-even well above 3.3%. Treat the toggle as a way of asking whether the question matters to you, not as a threshold. If your alternative is a savings account, pay cash. If it is a mortgage at a higher rate than the auto loan, pay the mortgage down and finance the car. The affordability calculator and monthly budget calculator handle the cash-flow side.

Who should lease, who should buy, who should pay cash

Most guides hedge at this point. We will not.

Lease if

You drive at or below the allowance and can prove it from your service history; the manufacturer is subventing the residual on the exact model you want; you want the car for a defined period with no intention of keeping it. Check for subvention by setting the contract buyout against a realistic three-year valuation, as above. If the buyout sits well over the car's likely market value, the deal is genuinely subsidized. Leasing also suits anyone buying into a fast-moving technology: EV retention runs 30–45% over five years against 45–55% for a compact or mid-size SUV, and a lease transfers that residual risk to the lender. Paying someone else to hold an uncertain asset is rational when the uncertainty is real.

Finance if

You intend to keep the car past the end of the loan. That is the whole case and nearly sufficient on its own — the six-year table turns on twelve payment-free months, and at eight or ten years of ownership buying wins by a margin no lease approaches. It also applies if you drive above the allowance, if your circumstances might change inside three years, or if you want the option to sell at any moment without penalty.

Pay cash if

The money is genuinely spare, your alternative use for it earns less than the loan rate after tax, and the purchase still leaves you a reserve. Cash removes $6,525 of interest from the benchmark car. It is the right answer more often than the internet suggests, because the returns usually quoted against it are not risk-free and the loan rate is.

Do not lease if

You drive high mileage, your income is variable, you tend to keep cars a long time, or you are being sold on the payment rather than the price.

Business use and tax treatment

For a business the answer can invert, and the treatment is genuinely different rather than merely presented differently.

A leased vehicle used for business is generally an operating expense, with the business-use proportion of the payments deductible in the year incurred. A purchased vehicle is a capital asset, recovered through depreciation deductions over a schedule set by the tax code, sometimes with accelerated first-year allowances. Leasing therefore tends to produce a smoother, earlier deduction; buying can produce a larger one in year one where an accelerated allowance applies. Luxury-vehicle caps, lease inclusion amounts and business-use thresholds all bear on the outcome, and they change.

There is also a cash-flow argument unrelated to tax: a lease keeps capital in the business, which matters more to a growing company than the total-cost difference on one vehicle. None of this is advice. The rules depend on your jurisdiction, entity type, the vehicle's weight and class, and how much of its use is genuinely business use. Speak to your accountant before letting tax treatment decide the question, and be skeptical of any dealer who raises it first. Frequently asked questions

Is it better to lease or buy a car?
Buying is better for most drivers, because at the end you hold an asset and a lease leaves you holding nothing. On our benchmark $34,000 SUV, two consecutive three-year leases cost $44,483 over six years and return nothing, while financing over 60 months and keeping the car costs $43,605 and leaves $12,069 of vehicle. The monthly cash flow is nearly identical. Leasing wins when the manufacturer subsidizes the residual and you stay inside the mileage cap.
What is a money factor?
A money factor is the interest rate on a lease, written as a small decimal rather than a percentage. Multiply it by 2,400 for the approximate APR: 0.00250 is about 6%. It is applied to the sum of the adjusted capitalised cost and the residual value, not to a declining balance, so the rent charge stays flat all term. On our benchmark car, 0.00250 produces $128.45 a month, or $4,624 over 36 months.
What happens at the end of a car lease?
You choose one of three things: return the car, buy it at the contract residual, or roll into a new lease. Return it and the car is inspected for excess wear, you settle any mileage overage, and you usually pay a disposition fee — commonly $350 to $500, and $395 in our benchmark. Buy it and the disposition fee is normally waived. The one thing that never happens is a refund: a lease holds no equity to give back.
Can you buy your leased car at the end?
Yes. Every lease names a buyout price: the residual set at signing, plus any purchase-option fee and sales tax. Whether it is a good deal depends entirely on the gap between that residual and the car's real market value. On our benchmark a 57% residual is $19,380 against a projected three-year value of $19,652 — $272 apart, so there is nothing to capture. At a subvented 64% residual the buyout would be $21,760 for a $19,652 car.
Is leasing a car throwing money away?
No, though the phrase persists. A lease charges you for the depreciation you consume, and a buyer pays exactly the same depreciation — it simply arrives silently, as a lower resale value. On our benchmark the lessee is charged $14,620 over three years while the buyer suffers $14,348 of real depreciation. The genuine difference is the rent charge, the acquisition and disposition fees, and the fact that a serial lessee never reaches the years when a paid-off car is nearly free to run.
What credit score do you need to lease a car?
Leasing is usually harder to qualify for than a loan, because the lender is exposed to the car's future value as well as to your payments. Captive finance arms grade applicants into tiers and reserve the advertised money factor for the top tier, which for most lenders begins somewhere in the low 700s, though every lender sets its own bands. A weaker tier rarely means rejection; it means a higher money factor, and that is where the cost hides.
Can you negotiate the price of a leased car?
Yes, and it is the most effective single thing you can do. The capitalised cost is simply the negotiated selling price, so every dollar off the price cuts both the depreciation portion and the rent charge. The money factor is negotiable too, within your credit tier, since dealers may mark it up over the lender's buy rate. Residual value is the one figure you cannot move. Settle the price first, and discuss the payment only afterwards.
What happens if you go over the mileage on a lease?
You pay a per-mile charge on every mile above the allowance, typically 15 to 30 cents, settled when you hand the car back. Our benchmark uses 25 cents against a 12,000-mile allowance. Drive 15,000 miles a year and you finish a three-year lease 9,000 miles over: $2,250. At 20,000 miles a year the bill is $6,000, roughly 27% on top of the whole cost of the lease. Prepaid miles are cheaper but not refundable.
