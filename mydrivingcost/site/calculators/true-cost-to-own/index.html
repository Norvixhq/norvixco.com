How the true cost to own is calculated

The “cost” of a car is not its price and it is not the monthly payment. It is every dollar the vehicle takes out of your life over the time you own it, minus whatever you get back when you sell. This calculator builds that number from six components, then divides by the miles you drive: your cost per mile.

  • Depreciation — purchase price minus projected resale value at the end of your ownership period. For most cars, the largest single cost.
  • Financing interest — the interest portion of your loan payments during the years you own the car.
  • Insurance — your annual premium across the ownership period.
  • Fuel or electricity — miles ÷ efficiency × energy price. For EVs, miles per kWh and your ¢/kWh rate.
  • Maintenance & repairs — routine service and expected repairs, rising as the car ages.
  • Taxes & fees — sales tax on the purchase plus annual registration and fees.
The formula, in one line
Total cost = Depreciation + Interest + Insurance + Fuel/Energy + Maintenance + Taxes & Fees  →  Cost per mile = Total ÷ (annual miles × years).

One line does more work than the rest. Only the interest portion of a loan payment is a cost. Principal converts cash into equity you recover at sale, and the part that evaporates is already charged under depreciation; counting the payment too would bill you twice. The model runs annually, depreciates on a declining balance, and escalates maintenance about 12% a year. Every default is sourced on our methodology page; the auto loan calculator decomposes a payment month by month.

A worked example

A $34,000 compact SUV, financed with $3,400 down at 7.2% APR over 60 months, driven 12,000 miles a year at 30 MPG with $4.00 gas, $2,496/yr full-coverage insurance, 7% sales tax, $700 in doc and dealer fees and $220/yr registration, held five years. The tax and the fees are financed into the loan, as they are in almost every real transaction, so the loan is written for $33,680 rather than $30,600:

The advertised payment on this car is about $670/month. The real cost of driving it is about $982/month — roughly 47% more, and about $18,700 across five years that the finance contract never mentions.

Two lines surprise people. Insurance at 21% is larger than fuel. And maintenance overtakes fuel by year four, as the warranty expires and the first real wear items come due. Both are invisible on the showroom floor; both, unlike the purchase price, are negotiable every year you own the car.

The payment illusion

Ask a driver what their car costs and they will quote the payment. On the benchmark that is $670.09 a month — a precise number, printed on a contract, debited on the same date each month. It is also wrong by about 47%. The true figure is $982 a month: a gap of $312 a month, $18,722 across five years. Here is where that money sits, and whether the finance agreement mentions it.

True cost of ownership$982—
Advertised loan payment$670.09—

The payment is larger than any single line above it, yet contains only $108.75 of genuine cost. The other $561 is principal — cash moving into the vehicle’s equity, most of which comes back at sale. What the payment omits is $873 a month of real spending. The agreement is not lying; it answers a different question from the one you asked.

Hence the damage a stretched loan does. A longer term lowers the payment and raises the interest, so the number you watch improves while the number that matters worsens — and it does nothing to the other five lines, which are indifferent to financing. Paying cash removes interest altogether: $52,402, or $0.87 a mile.

Why depreciation never sends a bill

Depreciation is the largest cost of owning the benchmark vehicle and the only one that never arrives as a demand for money. It happens quietly, on a forecourt you are not standing in, and you settle the account once, years later, as a smaller check than you expected. The default curve takes 20% in year one and 15% of the remainder thereafter:

The shape matters more than the total. Year one costs $6,800, or $567 a month — 85% of the entire loan payment, and not one cent of it billed to you. Year five costs $2,505, or $209. The first twelve months are roughly two and a half times as expensive as the fifth, and almost everything unpleasant about buying new sits in that one row.

It also governs equity, and here the benchmark is less comfortable than it looks. At the end of year one you owe $27,875 on a car worth $27,200: you are $675 underwater, on a deal with 10% down. That is not a badly structured loan — it is an ordinary one. First-year depreciation moves faster than early principal, because early payments are mostly interest, and the tax and fees financed into the loan bought you nothing you can resell. The benchmark surfaces at month 17 and stays above water for the rest of the term. Reduce the deposit or lengthen the term and that window stretches for years. Fixed costs, variable costs and the low-mileage trap

Of the benchmark’s $58,928, only the fuel line — $8,000 — moves when you drive more or less. Depreciation, insurance, interest, registration and most servicing are incurred by owning the vehicle, not by using it. That makes 86% of the total fixed, and it inverts the intuition nearly everybody has about mileage. Change only annual miles:

Annual miles5-year totalCost per yearCost per mile
6,000$54,928$10,986$1.83
12,000 (benchmark)$58,928$11,786$0.98
20,000$64,261$12,852$0.64

Driving 3.3 times as far raises the total bill by 17% and cuts the cost per mile by 65%. The low-mileage driver, who feels thrifty every time they walk to the shops, runs the most expensive car on this page at $1.83 a mile. The high-mileage driver, who winces at every fill-up, has the cheapest.

This is not an argument for driving more but about which car a low-mileage household should buy. At 6,000 miles a year fuel economy barely matters — the gap between 25 and 40 MPG is $360 a year — while depreciation and insurance are the whole story, so a cheaper, cheaply insured older car wins comfortably and an efficient new one is close to the worst answer available. At 20,000 miles the logic reverses. The cost per mile calculator isolates the relationship.

How long you keep it changes everything

Ownership length is the most powerful input on this page and the one people set most carelessly. Take the identical vehicle, financed and driven identically, and change only how long it is held:

Held forTotal costCost per yearCost per mileValue at end
3 years$39,973$13,324$1.11$19,652
5 years$58,928$11,786$0.98$14,199
8 years$84,788$10,599$0.88$8,720
10 years$102,401$10,240$0.85$6,300

The annual cost falls at every step and never rises — not because the later years are cheap, but because the two largest lines wind down. Depreciation collapses from $6,800 in year one to $2,130 in year six; interest stops at month sixty. Years six through ten cost an average of $8,695 a year, some 26% below the five-year average. That is where the return on the first five years of payments is collected.

It is also where received wisdom about repair bills falls apart. A three-year-old car has no repair costs and has cost $13,324 a year; an eight-year-old car has real repair costs and has cost $10,599 a year. The repair is visible and the depreciation is not, which is precisely why the visible one wins the argument.

Three years is the most expensive way to own a car short of leasing: $1.11 a mile against $0.85, roughly 30% more, paid permanently for the privilege of always driving something recent. The ten-year cost calculator extends this year by year; the lease versus buy calculator handles the case where a short cycle is genuinely what you want.

The two costs you can still change

Four of the six lines are settled the moment you sign: depreciation by what you bought, interest by the rate you accepted, tax by where you registered, maintenance by the badge on the bonnet. Two remain open every year.

Insurance is the second-largest cost and much the most volatile. The national average for full coverage is around $2,496 a year, but sources disagree widely — Insurify put it at $2,237 in July 2026, Insurance.com at $2,578 — and geography matters more than any average suggests. State figures run from $1,660 in Vermont to $3,999 in Louisiana: over five years, a spread of $11,695, more than the benchmark’s entire fuel bill. At the default premium, insurance costs 20.8 cents a mile against fuel’s 13.3, and it is the only large line you can renegotiate annually without changing the car. Our insurance estimator and insurance guide cover what moves a premium.

Energy is smaller than most people assume, and the electric arithmetic turns entirely on where you charge. The benchmark burns 400 gallons a year at 30 MPG — $1,600, or 14% of the total. An electric equivalent drawing 32 kWh per 100 miles at the plug uses 3,840 kWh a year, priced across a range of nearly three to one:

Charge at home and the same vehicle saves $4,640 over five years; charge in public car parks and the saving falls to $1,856, most of which is absorbed by the higher premium the calculator applies to EVs by default ($2,820 against $2,496, or $1,620 over five years). The energy saving is real, but it is a driveway benefit before it is a technology benefit.

Common mistakes people make

  • Comparing payments instead of costs. A longer loan lowers the payment but raises total interest and hides depreciation. Always compare all-in cost, not the monthly number.
  • Ignoring depreciation on a “cheap” car. A low price with terrible resale value can cost more per mile than a pricier car that holds its value.
  • Forgetting that costs scale differently. Depreciation and insurance are largely fixed; fuel and maintenance scale with miles. Low-mileage drivers pay more per mile than they expect.
  • Assuming an EV is automatically cheaper. Lower energy and maintenance costs are real, but a higher purchase price and steeper early depreciation can offset them.
  • Treating the trade-in as free money. Your next car's depreciation starts the day you drive off — the cycle repeats.
  • Judging a repair against the car’s value. A $2,500 repair on a car worth $8,000 sounds absurd and usually is not. The alternative is not keeping the $8,000; it is a new ownership cycle at roughly $982 a month. Ask how many months of driving the repair buys.
  • Setting the ownership period to match the loan term. The loan ends in year five; the car does not. Years six onwards are the cheapest you will ever drive, and selling at payoff hands that saving to somebody else.
  • Shopping the car and accepting the finance. Buyers will drive forty miles to save $500 on the price, then take whatever APR the desk offers. Two points on the benchmark loan is worth about $1,885.
The eighty-four-month loan
It is the most damaging product in car retail, sold as an act of generosity. Stretching the benchmark loan from sixty to eighty-four months cuts the payment from $670.09 to $511.62 — a 24% reduction costing $2,771 in extra interest, and that is before the higher rate lenders charge on long paper. The interest is the smaller problem. At the end of year two the sixty-month loan leaves you owing $21,638 on a car worth $23,120; the eighty-four-month loan leaves you owing $25,715. Still underwater at year three, and clear only part-way through year four. None of that shows in the total, because it costs nothing until something forces a sale.

One external check is worth holding against those ranges. Our benchmark returns 98 cents at 12,000 miles — but normalize it to AAA’s mileage and it falls to 81.2 cents a mile, or $12,186 a year, about 5% above AAA’s $11,577 average. Where they differ is instructive. AAA charges depreciation at $4,334 a year and finance at $1,131; we charge $3,960 and $1,305 — higher on finance because we charge interest on the sales tax and fees financed into the loan, which AAA does not. AAA models a clean-record driver on a national-average policy; we use the full-coverage average of $2,496, which is higher. Neither is more correct, and every field here is editable because you are not an average.

What this calculator deliberately leaves out

A cost model is defined as much by what it refuses to count as by what it includes. Five things are missing, each for a stated reason.

  • Parking, tolls, congestion charges and fines. These vary by two orders of magnitude between a rural household and a city one, and none is a property of the vehicle. A national average would make every result slightly wrong rather than usefully wrong. Add your own to the registration field.
  • Cleaning, valeting and accessories. Discretionary, unbounded, and less a cost of ownership than a cost of taste.
  • Opportunity cost, unless you ask for it. The return you give up by tying capital into a depreciating asset is real, and for a cash buyer substantial. It is off by default because the rate is a personal assumption, and applying one silently would inflate every result by an amount you never chose.
  • Extended warranties, gap insurance and dealer add-ons. Priced by the seller rather than by a market, wildly variable, often negative in value. If you bought one, add it to first-year maintenance.
  • The cost of not owning a car. This is a single-vehicle model. For a household weighing a second car against occasional rentals, that comparison matters more, and this page cannot make it.

Two limitations deserve stating too. Maintenance is modeled as smooth 12% annual growth, whereas real spending is lumpy — three quiet years followed by a $2,000 one. Over five years the average holds; in any single year it may be badly wrong, and the maintenance cost calculator models the pattern in detail. And resale value is a forecast: curves shifted materially in 2021 and again in 2023, so any projection five years out is an educated guess wearing decimal places.

What to actually do with the number

A cost-of-ownership figure is only worth calculating if it changes a decision. Four it should change:

  • Set your budget from the all-in figure, not the payment. Whatever share of income you are prepared to spend on a car, apply it to $982 and not to $670.09. A payment you can meet comfortably, on a car whose true cost is 47% higher, is the ordinary route into being unable to afford a vehicle you already own.
  • Compare vehicles on total first, then on cost per mile. The total answers “which is cheaper to own?”; the cost per mile answers “which is cheaper to use?”. They disagree more often than you would expect.
  • Decide the ownership period before you buy. Three years against ten is worth about 26 cents a mile, which dwarfs anything you will win at the negotiating table. It also changes which car you should buy: a long hold rewards reliability and a flat depreciation curve.
  • Run it again every year. Insurance renews, fuel prices move, maintenance escalates, the curve flattens. The number that made a car look expensive in year one looks very different in year six, when the keep-or-replace decision actually arrives.

The figure is not a verdict; it is a price, and a great many things are worth ninety-six cents a mile. The point of computing it is to know what you are buying and stop being surprised in year four.

Frequently asked questions

How is the true cost to own a car calculated?
We add six categories over your ownership period — depreciation, financing interest, insurance, fuel or electricity, maintenance and repairs, and taxes and fees — then divide the total by the miles you drive to get your cost per mile. Every input is editable, so the result reflects your situation, not a generic average.
Is depreciation really the biggest cost of owning a car?
For most new vehicles, yes. A typical car loses roughly 15–25% of its value in year one and about 55–65% over five years. That lost value is usually the largest ownership cost — often more than fuel, insurance and maintenance combined — yet it never arrives as a monthly bill, which is exactly why people overlook it.
Does the calculator work for electric and hybrid cars?
Yes. Switch the powertrain to Hybrid or Electric and the tool models energy using efficiency (miles per kWh for EVs) and your electricity price, and adjusts typical maintenance and depreciation defaults. You can override any of them.
What is a good cost per mile for a car?
For a typical gas car driven 12,000 miles a year over five years, all-in costs often land between about 55 and 90 cents per mile. Efficient compact cars sit at the low end; trucks, luxury models and low-mileage drivers sit higher because fixed costs are spread over fewer miles.
Should I include opportunity cost?
Optionally. Opportunity cost estimates the investment return you give up by tying cash up in a car. It matters most for cash buyers and expensive vehicles. It’s off by default because it’s a personal assumption — turn it on in Advanced settings to fold it into the total.
Why is my cost per mile so high if I barely drive?
Because most of the cost of a car is fixed. Depreciation, insurance, interest, registration and scheduled servicing accrue whether the vehicle moves or not, and on our benchmark they account for 86% of the total. Fuel is the only line that shrinks meaningfully with mileage. Drop from 12,000 miles a year to 6,000 and the five-year total falls only 7%, from $58,928 to $54,928, while the cost per mile nearly doubles to $1.83. Low-mileage households should buy a cheaper, cheaply insured car rather than an efficient one.
Does paying cash for a car actually save money?
Usually yes, though less than the headline interest figure suggests. Paying cash for the benchmark vehicle removes $6,525 of interest and takes the five-year total from $58,928 to $52,402, or $0.87 a mile. Against that, $34,000 of capital is now tied up in an asset that is losing value, and the return you forgo on it is a genuine cost. Switch on opportunity cost in Advanced assumptions to see the effect. Cash wins clearly whenever the loan rate exceeds what your savings earn, which at 7.2% APR is usually the case.
How long should I keep a car to minimize cost?
Longer than most people do, and the curve moves in one direction only. Held three years the benchmark costs $13,324 a year; held five, $11,786; held eight, $10,599; held ten, $10,240. The annual cost falls at every step because depreciation and interest, the two largest lines, wind down faster than maintenance rises. The steepest improvement comes once the loan clears in year five. Beyond about ten years the curve flattens and the decision becomes one about reliability and safety rather than money.
Sources & assumptions

The defaults on this page are durable national reference points, not live market quotes. They are chosen to stay reasonable across years rather than to track this week’s prices, and every one of them is a field you can change — your real numbers depend on your vehicle, ZIP code, driving pattern and credit. The model that consumes them is documented in full on the methodology page.
