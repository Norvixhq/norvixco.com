Repair or Replace: How to Evaluate a Roof

There is a real answer to this question, and it is not always the more expensive one.

2 min readUpdated 2026-08-05Timber Roofing & Exteriors

On this page

  1. The four factors that actually decide it
  2. Cases where repair is usually right
  3. Cases where replacement is usually right
  4. Questions worth asking whoever inspected it

Plenty of roofs get replaced that only needed a repair, and plenty get repaired repeatedly when they should have been replaced two years ago. Both are expensive mistakes. Here is how to think about it.

The four factors that actually decide it

  1. How widespread the damage is. Damage confined to one slope or a few details is a repair. Damage scattered across every elevation is not.
  2. How much life the roof has left. Fixing a detail on a roof with twelve good years ahead of it is money well spent. Doing the same work on a roof with two years left is not.
  3. Whether the material can be matched. Sometimes a repair is technically sound but the color match is poor enough that appearance becomes the deciding factor.
  4. What is underneath. If tear-off is going to reveal soft decking or old water damage, that changes the arithmetic entirely.

Cases where repair is usually right

A single failed pipe boot. Wind damage confined to one windward slope. A flashing detail at a chimney that was sealed instead of built correctly. A valley that has worn through while the rest of the field is sound. In each case the roof is fundamentally healthy and one component has failed.

Cases where replacement is usually right

Hail bruising across multiple slopes. Repeated leaks in different locations over consecutive seasons. Bare shingle mat where the granules are gone entirely. A visible sag in the roof plane. Any situation where you have already paid for two or three repairs in three years.

Three leaks in three seasons in three different places is a roof telling you it is finished. One leak is an event.

Questions worth asking whoever inspected it

  • Can you show me a photograph of the actual failure point?
  • How many years do you think the rest of the roof has left, and why?
  • If we repair this, what is the most likely next thing to fail?
  • What would you do if it were your house?

The answers tell you a great deal. Someone who can show you the specific failure and reason through the remaining life of the roof is thinking about your roof. Someone who moves straight to replacement without either is thinking about something else.

If storm damage is involved and you have a policy, get the damage documented before you make a decision either way. Documentation close in time to the event is more useful than documentation months later.
