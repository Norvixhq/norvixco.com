Most North Texas homes are covered in architectural asphalt shingles, and most of them will need attention somewhere between year twelve and year twenty-five. Hail moves that timeline. So does poor ventilation, a bad original install, or a decade of 100-degree Augusts baking the granules off the south-facing slopes.

We work on single-family homes across Dallas–Fort Worth and the surrounding communities — from 1970s ranch houses in Richardson and Garland to the steep, cut-up rooflines that came with the Frisco and Prosper build-out. Different eras, different problems. A licensed inspector looks at yours before anybody quotes a number.

When residential roofing is worth looking into

Granules collecting in the gutters

Shingles shed their protective surface as they age. A handful of granules after a new install is normal. Piles of them in year fifteen are not.

Ceiling stains or attic daylight

Water rarely enters directly above the stain. It travels. That is why the source has to be found from the roof and the attic, not guessed from below.

Shingles that look bruised or dented

Hail impact often shows as soft dark circles where granules were knocked loose rather than obvious holes.

Curling, cupping, or lifted edges

Usually heat and age, sometimes a ventilation problem underneath. Either way the seal strip is no longer doing its job in high wind.

A roof that is simply old

If you have no idea how old it is and you have lived there a while, that by itself is worth a free inspection.

Causes

What causes it

Hail

North Texas sits in one of the most active hail corridors in the country. Impact damage is the single most common reason a DFW roof gets replaced early.

Wind

Straight-line winds ahead of a squall line break shingle seals and peel edges, especially on the windward slope and along rakes and ridges.

Heat cycling

Surface temperatures on a dark shingle roof in a Texas July are brutal, and the daily expansion and contraction ages the mat and the sealant.

Trapped attic heat and moisture

Under-ventilated attics cook shingles from below and shorten their service life from the side you cannot see.

Installation shortcuts

Over-driven nails, missing starter course, skipped ice-and-water at valleys, reused flashing. We find all of it, most often on a roof somebody else put on cheap.

Our inspection

How we look at it

  • Walk the roof where it is safe to do so, and use drone imagery where it is not
  • Check field shingles, ridge caps, valleys, rakes, eaves, and every penetration
  • Look at flashing at walls, chimneys, skylights, and pipe boots
  • Check gutters and downspouts for granule load and drainage problems
  • Go into the attic where accessible to look for daylight, staining, and ventilation
  • Photograph everything and walk you through the photos in plain language

North Texas conditions

North Texas asks a lot of a roof: hail in spring, straight-line wind ahead of the storms, then four months of relentless heat. A roof system that would last twenty-five years in a mild climate often does not get there here, and the failure usually starts at the details — valleys, flashing, boots, and ventilation — rather than in the middle of the field.

Options

Materials and approaches

Architectural asphalt shingles

The default for good reason in this market — strong wind ratings, deep shadow lines, and a wide color range that suits North Texas brick.

Impact-resistant shingles

Class 4 rated products are worth a conversation in a hail corridor. Ask us what the real-world trade-offs are before you assume they are always the answer.

Three-tab shingles

Still found on older rentals and smaller structures. We will install them where they make sense and tell you when they do not.

Underlayment and accessories

Synthetic underlayment, ice-and-water membrane in valleys and around penetrations, new pipe boots, new flashing, and proper ridge ventilation.

Ventilation correction

Balanced intake and exhaust addressed as part of the roof rather than treated as a separate trade.

Flashing and penetration package

New step, counter, and apron flashing plus fresh boots on every vent, rather than reusing what is there.

In detail

What North Texas does to a roof, in order

Spring brings the hail. It knocks granules off the surface and fractures the mat underneath, and it rarely leaks straight away — it simply moves the roof's expiry date forward by several years without telling anyone.

Ahead of those same storms comes straight-line wind, which does the damage homeowners consistently underestimate. Gusts get under a shingle's leading edge, break the factory seal strip, and fold the shingle back. It settles down again looking completely normal. It is no longer bonded to anything.

Then comes the heat. Surface temperatures on a dark roof through a North Texas July are punishing, and the daily expansion and contraction works every sealant joint and ages the asphalt mat. Attic air sits against the underside of the deck all afternoon and ages the same shingles from the other side.

A roof system rated well past twenty years in a mild climate frequently does not get there here. And when it fails, it almost never fails in the middle of a slope. It fails at a boot, a valley, a piece of flashing, or a ventilation problem — the details.

Reading your own roof from the ground

You cannot diagnose a roof from the driveway, but you can tell whether it deserves a closer look. Stand back far enough to see a whole slope at once, ideally in low-angle morning or evening light, which shows surface texture that midday sun flattens out.

Look for uneven tone across a plane, which usually means granule loss. Look for lines that should be straight and are not — sagging ridges, wavy courses, a dip in the field. Look at the ridge and hip caps, which are more exposed and show impact before field shingles do. Then look at things that are not the roof at all: dented gutters and downspouts, dented vent housings, and flattened AC condenser fins are all strong evidence of hail.

What you cannot see from down there is the entire category that matters most. Bruising, broken wind seals, cracked pipe boots, and flashing that was caulked instead of built are all invisible at that distance. That is what the free inspection is for.

Different eras, different problems

The Metroplex contains roughly seventy years of residential construction, and roof problems sort fairly reliably by vintage. Homes from the 1960s and 1970s across Garland, Mesquite, Hurst, and Bedford tend toward simple hip geometry on single-story footprints — easy to inspect and straightforward to replace, but usually on their second or third roof, with the small components long past their service life and prior work of wildly varying quality hiding underneath.

The 1980s and 1990s build-out through Plano, Arlington, and Carrollton brought two-story homes with steeper front elevations and more articulation. Damage on the upper slopes goes unseen from the ground for years.

The 2000s onward — Frisco, Allen, Prosper, Celina, Rockwall — brought large, heavily cut-up rooflines with high valley and penetration counts. Those are impressive from the street, harder to inspect safely, and they concentrate water into details that have to be built correctly. They are also new enough that entire subdivisions reach the end of their original roof within a season or two of each other.

From our inspections

What we keep finding on North Texas roofs

Ventilation nobody looked at

Soffit intake blocked solid with insulation while turbines spin on the ridge. The attic cannot breathe and the shingles cook from below.

Chimneys with no cricket

A wide chimney with nothing diverting water around its uphill side. A recurring cause of chronic leaks on brick-heavy North Texas homes.

Boots at the end of their life

Rubber collars split by a decade of UV, on roofs that otherwise have years left.

Layovers hiding bad decking

Two or three layers of shingles, with whatever went wrong under the first one still sitting there.

Satellite mounts abandoned in place

Old dishes removed and the fasteners left, sealed with something that gave up years ago.

Questions

Residential Roofing FAQs

How long does a roof last in Dallas–Fort Worth?

Architectural asphalt shingles are commonly rated well past twenty years, but North Texas hail and heat frequently shorten the real service life. Age alone does not decide it — condition does. That is what the inspection is for.

Do I need a full replacement or can it be repaired?

It depends on how widespread the damage is, how old the roof is, and whether matching material is realistic. If a repair will genuinely hold, we will tell you that. We would rather do a good repair now than sell a replacement you did not need.

Is the inspection really free?

Yes. Free inspections and free estimates, with no obligation to schedule work.

How long does a residential roof replacement take?

Many single-family homes are a one to two day job once materials are on site, depending on size, pitch, complexity, and weather. We will give you a realistic window with your estimate.

Will you work on my house if I am outside the Metroplex?

Often, yes. We regularly work across North Texas and travel roughly two hours from Dallas for the right project. Call with your address and we will give you a straight answer.
